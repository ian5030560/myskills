## How to use

The MMAT is for empirical studies only. It does not fit reviews, theoretical papers, economic evaluations or diagnostic accuracy studies.

1. Answer the two screening questions. If the answer to either is No or Can't tell, the paper may not be an empirical study, or further appraisal may not be feasible or appropriate; say so in the report.
2. Pick the category (or categories) that match the study design from the description of its methods, and answer its five criteria only. Guide for the quantitative designs:
   - Researchers assign the intervention, more than one group, random allocation: category 2.
   - Non-randomised trial, before-and-after or time-series study, cohort study, case-control study, analytical cross-sectional study, or another design that compares groups: category 3.
   - No comparison of outcomes between interventions or exposures (case series, case report, descriptive cross-sectional study, survey, ecological study): category 4.
   - Qualitative studies: category 1.
   - A mixed methods study (at least one qualitative and one quantitative method, each used rigorously, and integrated by design) is appraised on category 5, category 1, and one of categories 2, 3 or 4 for the quantitative part.
3. Answer each criterion with **Yes**, **No** or **Can't tell**, and write the paper's location and the basis. Never assume what the researchers did. Can't tell means the paper does not report the information needed for Yes or No, or reports it unclearly; write what is missing in "still to confirm".
4. **Do not calculate an overall score.** Present each criterion's answer. Do not call a study high or low quality from a count.

## Screening questions (all study types)

- S1. Are there clear research questions?
- S2. Do the collected data make it possible to answer the research questions?

## 1. Qualitative studies

- 1.1 Is the qualitative approach suitable for answering the research question?
- 1.2 Are the data collection methods adequate for the question?
- 1.3 Are the findings properly derived from the data?
- 1.4 Is the interpretation of the results well supported by the data?
- 1.5 Do the data sources, collection, analysis and interpretation fit together coherently?

## 2. Quantitative randomised controlled trials

- 2.1 Was randomisation carried out appropriately?
- 2.2 Were the groups comparable at baseline?
- 2.3 Are outcome data complete?
- 2.4 Were the people assessing outcomes blind to the intervention?
- 2.5 Did participants stick to the intervention they were assigned?

## 3. Quantitative non-randomised studies

- 3.1 Are the participants representative of the target population?
- 3.2 Are the measurements of the outcome and of the intervention (or exposure) appropriate?
- 3.3 Are outcome data complete?
- 3.4 Are confounders accounted for in the design and analysis?
- 3.5 During the study, was the intervention delivered (or the exposure present) as intended?

## 4. Quantitative descriptive studies

- 4.1 Is the sampling strategy relevant to the research question?
- 4.2 Is the sample representative of the target population?
- 4.3 Are the measurements appropriate?
- 4.4 Is the risk of non-response bias low?
- 4.5 Is the statistical analysis appropriate for the research question?

## 5. Mixed methods studies

- 5.1 Is there an adequate reason for using a mixed methods design?
- 5.2 Are the qualitative and quantitative components effectively integrated?
- 5.3 Are the results of that integration adequately interpreted?
- 5.4 Are differences and inconsistencies between the quantitative and qualitative results adequately addressed? (Answer Yes if there are no differences. Reporting a difference is not enough; it has to be explained.)
- 5.5 Does each component meet the quality criteria of its own tradition? (Judge each component with its own category's criteria. The mixed methods study cannot be rated better than its weakest component.)
