# Content judgement rules

Read the paper itself and judge each item below against the user's research question. These rules double as the self-check list: after writing, check the report against every rule and fix what fails.

Whether the study design is rigorous is **not** judged here; the standard checklists of the external assessment cover that.

## Rules

Each item has one of three outcomes: **ok**, **concern**, or **not enough information**. Every outcome is followed by the paper's location (section, page, table or figure). If the paper gives too little to decide, write "not enough information" and say what is missing. Never guess.

1. **Source and version can be verified.** The title, authors, year, DOI and publication details (volume and pages, or proceedings) on the PDF agree with each other and with the DOI records in the metrics output. The article type the paper claims matches what it actually does. Any correction or update notice is looked up (the metrics output lists Crossref correction and update records under `updates`). Note mismatches, a version that differs from the formal one, and anything that cannot be checked.
2. **The content answers the user's research question.** Compare the study subjects, setting, method and measured results with the user's question. Say which part of the question the paper addresses, which part it leaves open, and where the paper's own question differs from the user's. Both being about the same technology (for example "AI") does not mean they study the same thing.
3. **The evidence supports the authors' claims.** Check the data source, the way groups or methods are compared, and the limits of the analysis. For each main claim, find the data, result or argument offered for it. Keep what the results show apart from what the authors say they mean. Note claims stronger than their evidence (for example causal wording for correlational data) and results reported without the numbers behind them.
4. **The measurement fits.** The measures, instruments, datasets or outcomes stand for what the claim is about. Tell apart what people think (attitudes, satisfaction), how a task went right now (scores, completion time, whether tools were allowed) and what they can do on their own (new tasks without the tool, delayed tests); these answer different questions. Note proxies used as if they were the real thing and measures not described well enough to check.
5. **The scope and the unit of inference fit.** Say who or what the results cover (population, setting, period, unit of analysis) and whether the user's question lies inside it. Check what the headline number counts (for example papers analysed versus people studied). Note generalization beyond the sample, conclusions drawn at a different level than the data, and results that reflect attention or interest being read as effects.

## AI-generated summaries

Apply this check when the paper, its page or its publisher interface contains an AI-written summary, or when the user supplied an AI-generated summary or overview of the paper.

- The source cards or citations it shows (title, authors, year) correspond to the actual article.
- Compare each statement with the paper's own text. Note every statement the paper does not make or contradicts, every important caveat the summary drops, and any change in the unit, the method (for example data analysis described as a trial) or the scope of the claim. Cite the location of the original statement.
- Method details and numbers come from the full text, never from the summary. A summary is never used as evidence in place of the paper.

## Writing the judgement

- One short paragraph or bullet per item, outcome first, location after.
- State only what the paper shows. Keep your own inference marked as inference.
- Do not give a number, grade, star or percentage for any item.
